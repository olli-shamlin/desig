How the glossary is organized

There are three types of items in the glossary:

  • terms

    Each term will (eventually) have an individual page where the definition of the term is found. You can access a term's page from both the terms and acronyms indexes. See the "What's on a term's page" section below for more detail on individual term pages.

    As we identify terms we want to include in our glossary, I will likely put them on the pending index initially. When I have added the definition content and it appears in the terms and/or acronyms index, I will remove it from the pending index.

  • synonyms

    When a term has one or more synonyms, the term's synonym(s) will also appear in the terms index. Each synonym entry found in the terms index will be of the form

    word: synonym of term

    The "term" will be a link that you click/tap on to view that term's definition page.

  • acronyms

    Given the prevalent use of acronyms in "geek speak", I decided to create a separate index for them. I did try "treating" acronyms like terms in terms of indexing. (I.e., having both terms and acronyms appear in the same index.) That didn't "feel" good enough to me; the acronyms didn't "stand out" visually in a meaningful way to me.

    Don't be surprised: Acronyms of terms on the pending index will have a term page but that term page will only show the term's associated acronym.

Navigating the Terms index

The terms index is subdivided by the letters of the alphabet. At the top of terms index page is a "subindex" containing all the letters of the alphabet. The letters in this subindex are formatted (a.k.a. "styled") in one of the following ways:

  • Bold: Indicates the terms and synonyms visible on this page start with this letter
  • Blue and underlined: indicates there are terms or synonyms in the glossary that start with this letter. Click/tap on a letter to see the terms/synonyms that start with that letter.
  • Grey: Indicates there are currently no terms or synonyms in the glossary that start with this letter.

Note: I don't like how this subindex currently gets "chopped off" on small screens (e.g., smartphones). You currently have to scroll horizontally to the right when that happens. I've already a few ways to address this issue, but haven't found a better alternative yet. I have a few more ideas to look into; I need a few more hours to explore them further. Stay tuned.

What's on a term's page

A term page can have up to four pieces of information on it:

  • definition: an explanation of the term written by yours truly.

    In an ideal world, I will write an explanation for every term in the glossary. Until we live in an ideal world, I may only include references on a term's page. If you encounter a term with references but no explanation and find the references insufficient to understand the term, please let me know (ideally in an email; that will allow me to quickly/easily add an item to the "to do" list I maintain on my laptop of things I need to remember to do for our SIG.)

  • acronym: only appears when the term has an associate acronym.

  • synonyms: only appears when the term has one or more associated synonyms.

  • references: links to articles I found on other sites that provide (what I consider) reasonable definitions. See more on references in the section below.

More on references

I am conducting an experiment of sorts by including references to pages/articles I find via Internet searches. When a "computer geeks" needs to understand a technical term they are not familiar with, they will quickly/briefly do an Internet search to discover its meaning. One or more of the results on the first page of an internet search (e.g., Google Search) of "what is the meaning of 'term' in computer terms" typically has sufficient information the "computer geeks" is looking for; its equally likely that one or more of the results are not helpful. How does the "computer geek" know which is which?

As I am reviewing pages/articles I find using internet search queries similar to the one above, I am keeping the following questions in mind to help me decide whether or not I include the page/article on a term page:

  • How much technical jargon does the article use to explain the term?

    An explanation that is written in "plain English" is better than one that uses a lot of other technical terms. When reviewing pages/articles to include in our glossary, I try to stick with those that limit their use of other technical terms to those I think a general audience should be familiar with.

  • What is the scope of the article?

    Some of the pages/articles I consider cover more than just define/explain a term. I will include those in our glossary if the definition/explanation they include aligns with the other criteria I am using to vet pages/articles and the definition/explanation is clearly/obviously found at the beginning of the page/article.

    This has a practical implication for you when you look at the references in our glossary: If the page/article is long, you don't have to read it entirely; you should get "the gist" of the associated term's meaning from the first few paragraphs. Be on the look out for "What is a term" section headings too; you will see them in some of these longer pages/articles.

  • Who is the target audience?

    Ideally, I am looking for pages/articles that are targeted for what I think of as a "general" or "consumer" audience--as opposed to a digital tech professional or academicians. When some of the articles/pages returned by my search category are targeted for a "general/consumer" audience while others are targeted for a "professional/academic" audience, I will pick pages/articles targeted for the former and reject those targeted for the latter.

  • Who is the author/publisher of the article?

    Anyone that has access to the Internet can publish content these days; some are definitely better/more reputable that others. Discerning between the good and not-so-good is sometimes "more art than science." When I encounter an author/publisher I am not familiar with, I do spend some time trying to "sussing out" their credentials; if I can't find "about us" information on their site that satisfies me, then I consider how "professional" the site appears--which is arguably where the "art" comes in. I reject pages/articles on sites that appear "janky" (as kids today say.)

  • Is the article's explanation of the term accurate?

    I am already familiar with the terms we're including in our glossary making it relatively easy for me to discern if a page/article I am reviewing is reasonably accurate or not, how can you make that kind of determination about a page/article when you have know a prior knowledge related to the question you are trying to answer using Internet search results?

    When the pages/articles I am considering meet the criteria represented by the other questions in this list, I compare/contrast the pertinent content of the pages/articles. When I find three or more articles that are fairly similar, I assume those are more accurate than any of the pages/articles that are significantly different.
