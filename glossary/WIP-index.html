How the glossary is organized

There are three types of items in the glossary:

  • terms

    Each term will (eventually) have an individual page where the definition of the term is found. You can access a term's page from both the terms and acronyms indexes. See the "What's on a term's page" section below for more detail on individual term pages.

    As we identify terms we want to include in our glossary, I will likely to just put them on the pending index initially. It's taking me about 5-10 minutes minimum to draft an individual term page and we're starting with a "backlog" of terms introduced in previous discussions. So one way to think of the pending index is "David's 'to do' list" in continuing to add to the glossary incrementally over time.

  • synonyms

    When a term has one or more synonyms, the term's synonym(s) will also appear in the terms index. Each synonym entry found in the terms index will be of the form

    word: synonym of term

    The "term" will be a link that you can click/tap on to view that term's definition page.

  • acronyms

    Given the prevalent use of acronyms in "geek speak", I decided to create a separate index for them. I did try "treating" acronyms like terms in terms of indexing. (I.e., having both terms and acronyms appear in the same index.) That didn't "feel" good enough to me; the acronyms didn't "stand out" visually in a meaningful way to me.

    There is a "gotcha" with the way I am doing this: terms on the pending index that also have an associated acronym will have a term page but that term page will only show the term's associated acronym.

Navigating the Terms index

The terms index is subdivided by the letters of the alphabet. At the top of terms index page is a "subindex" containing all the letters of the alphabet. The letters in this subindex are formatted (a.k.a. "styled") in one of the following ways:

  • Bold: Indicates the terms and synonyms visible on this page start with this letter
  • Blue and underlined: indicates there are terms or synonyms in the glossary that start with this letter. Click/tap on a letter to see the terms/synonyms that start with that letter.
  • Grey: Indicates there are currently no terms or synonyms in the glossary that start with this letter.

Note that I don't like how this subindex currently gets "chopped off" on small screens (like smartphones). You have to scroll horizontally to the right when that happens. I've already a few ways to address this issue, but haven't found a better alternative yet. I have a few more ideas to look into; I need a few more hours to explore them further. Stay tuned.

What's on a term's page

A term page can have up to four pieces of information on it

  • Definition: an explanation of the term written by yours truly.

    In an ideal world, I will write an explanation for every term in the glossary. Until we live in an ideal world, I may only include references on a term's page. If you encounter a term with references but no explanation and find the references insufficient to understand the term, please let me know (ideally in an email; that will allow me to quickly/easily add an item to the "to do" list I maintain on my laptop of things I need to remember to do for our SIG.)

  • Acronym: only appears when the term has an associate acronym.
  • Synonyms: only appears when the term has one or more associated synonyms.
  • References: links to articles I found on other sites that provide (what I consider) reasonable definitions. See more on references in the section below.

More on references

Given that one of my aims is to help others learn how to find answers to their digital technology questions on the Internet, including links to articles I find on other sites for the terms in our glossary seems like a worthwhile thing to do. I frequently search for articles online when I'm preparing content I share with the groups; sometimes I need to clarify or confirm my own understanding. In terms of searching online for the definitions of digital technology terms, asking a search engine "what does 'term' mean in computer terms" usually gives me more than enough articles to get what I need. But not all articles are equal! Many are written for an audience with a fairly deep technical/academic understanding of digital technology or computer science. I've also encountered more than one article I consider inaccurate.

As I started to put the glossary section of this site together, I did spend some time searching the internet for definitions/explanations of terms to see how much effort it took me to find two to three references for a handful of terms that I think should be reasonably/mostly understandable by most of the members in the group. The articles I've found for some terms may be sufficient in and of themselves; i.e., I might not be adding much additional value by writing a "custom" definition for those terms. So you don't be surprised if you encounter some term pages that have references but no "custom" definitions.

When I review articles that I may include as references on a term page, I answer the following questions:

  • Who is the target audience?

    Many of the articles I encounter are written for digital technology professionals/academicians. Some such cases, I've found are suitable for the purpose of our glossary; I've already included a few and rejected a number of others.

  • Who is the author of the article?

    A number of the articles I have already included as reference on term pages were written/published by digital tech companies. Tech companies often include blogs on their websites where they share information about their product(s) and the related digital technology space they play in. E.g., many cybersecurity companies do this. These kind of articles are often targeted at the personal user/consumer--as opposed to the business user/consumer--and well suited for our purposes.

    That being said, these articles do sometimes include what I think of as a "soft sales pitch" for a product the company sells related to the article's topic. If/when I include such an article that does that, I am not attempting to suggest/recommend purchasing the product mentioned in the article.

  • Is the article accurate?

    Believe it or not, I often encounter articles on digital technology that are not accurate. Its possible for anyone with Internet access to put information out there on the Internet these days, and unfortunately, there are people posting content attempting to explain aspects of digital technology (like what it is, how it works, and/or how to use it) that are just not correct. (I don't think the people who are publishing this inaccuracies are doing so with any explicit intention to spread misinformation/disinformation; I just think they are don't know the technology they are attempting to explain well enough to explain it correctly/clearly.

  • How much technical jargon does the article use?

    Since I'm suggesting articles to help you understand the meaning of terms, I want those articles don't use too many other technical terms. While most do, I'm attempting to choose those that mostly use additional technical terms that I think everyone will benefit from knowing--i.e., they are in the set of terms that belong in our glossary.

  • How big is the scope of the article?

    The scope of an ideal reference article would be limited to defining the related term. Articles often go beyond that. E.g., I've encountered articles that also describe how to use technology associated with the term or give some broader analysis of its merits/weaknesses. When I am vetting these kinds of articles for use as term references, I am trying to stick to those that define/explain the term in the first few paragraphs of the article; hopefully that allows you to get the intended value out of that article as term refence and stop reading if you want to if you experience it "going into the weeds."
