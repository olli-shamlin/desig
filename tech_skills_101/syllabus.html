Tech Skills 101: Syllabus

Overview

Why should we invest time/effort into learning about digital technology and developing new skills? Because there are too many things in life that now involve--dare I say "require"--using digital technology.

How many days in a row could you go without using at least one of your digital devices to get some task or personal business done? Could you go a week with your desktop/laptop, smartphone, and table all turned off?

How frequently do get stuck/confused/hesitant/uncertain/frustrated when doing things with your digital devices? Most times (ie multiple times per week), more than once a month? Do you think you are getting value from your digital devices that is commensurate with the monetary costs you pay for them (both cost of the devices themselves and fees you pay for software and services?)

  • To bolster your ability/proficiency with fundamental/basic "hands on" skills

Hardware

Aims

Primary goal: Provide you with an understanding of the most basic/fundamental characteristics of digital computation.

Motivation/Rationale: Most of the challenges/struggles we have when using digital devices are not due to or caused by hardware itself; instead they are due to the software you are using.

Objectives

  • Describe the primary subsystems of computational devices
  • Explain how the processor(s), memory, and storage contained in digital devices work together as the lowest-level computational engine of digital technology
  • Explain how the power and capacity of computational devices is measured
  • Gestures: Introduction to pointing devices and their use

Scope/Outline

  1. Internals: overview of what's inside your laptop/iPad/iPhone
  2. "The Brain": Processors, Memory, and Storage
  3. Interactivity
    1. Survey of the components in laptops/tablets/phones used for human-computer interaction
    2. Gestures: the basics of using a mouse, trackpad, and touchscreens
  4. Connectivity
    1. Wired vs wireless connections
    2. Common peripherals
  5. Sizing: How the processing power/capacity of hardware is measured

Software

Aims

This section aims enable you to problem solve when you get stuck using an app by providing you with an understanding of basic computer science constructs/concepts. The intent is to enable you to think more critically/tactically in order to get "unstuck" independently and/or ask for help from "tech support" type people in your social network.

Objectives

  • Explain what software is, how software/apps are created, and what happens "under the covers" when you use an app
  • Describe the relationship between an app when executing/running and the processor/memory/storage
  • Describe the the role and responsibilities of operating systems with an emphasis/focus on select OS subsystems (e.g., the file system, user interface, and system settings) that you directly interact with
  • More on data: Survey of common data structures (with examples of how they appear in apps)

Scope/Outline

  1. What is software
    1. App lifecycle
    2. Software release lifecycle
    3. Concept: Configuration: Customizing your device/apps
  2. Operating Systems
    1. OS subsystems: the role and responsibilities of operating systems
    2. Kinds/types of operating systems
    3. Relationship between apps, os, and hardware
  3. The File System
    1. Concept: When do apps save data to storage? (i.e., file-based apps vs database-based apps
    2. file system layout -- os vs user spaces; standard folder locations
    3. file metadata
    4. folders
      1. special OS folders
      2. smart folders
      3. concept of "recents"
    5. file manager apps
      1. standard functions: open, search, copy, move, delete, modify file/folder properties
    6. How to find a file you previously saved
    7. Concept:auto-save
    8. Concept:file revisions
    9. "cleaning" storage
    10. backing up
  4. User Interface (UI): How we interact with software
    1. Controls
    2. Common icons
    3. Types of windows: app, dialogs: popup
    4. The Desktop
    5. Common actions/operations (e.g., copy/cut+paste, drag-and-drop ("move"), resizing windows, ...)
  5. Settings

The Internet

Aims

The Internet is the foundational technology that enables us to do things "online." It enables a distributed repository of human knowledge and services (generically referred to as "the Cloud") as well as a mechanism for communicating/interacting with other people globally.

This Internet is built with computers and software. This part of the course focuses on describing the Internet works. using the concepts/constructs introduced in the hardware and software sections of the course.

Objectives

  • Describe the key/fundamental role the Internet plays in the digital landscape:
    1. As a global communication network
    2. As a distributed knowledge and service repository
    3. As the foundational technology for cloud (ie, distributed and virtualized) computing
  • Describe the Internet's infrastructure (i.e., how was the Internet built?)
  • Explain how the Internet works via basic/common use cases (e.g., viewing a web page using a browser, sending/receiving email, making an online purchase, etc)
  • Provide a technical explanation of the differences between the Internet, the Web, and the Dark Web
  • Describe the characteristics/properties of data in/on the Internet
  • A working understanding of security/privacy considerations when using cloud technology

Scope/Outline

  1. Introduction to computer networks/networking and data centers (and the role they play in the internet)
  2. Sites vs Services
  3. Browsers: survey of common features and UI elements
  4. Email
  5. Cookies & Caches
  6. URLs

"Street Smarts"

Overview

Now that we have a working understanding how the foundational technologies bring the digital world into being, we can explore how to explore/access the digital domain effectively and safely. I.e., pursue our individual needs & wants/interests/desires in ways that align with our personal priorities & values while maintaining our overall well-being and integrity.

The astronomical trove of sites and services on the Internet is a smorgasbord of human knowledge and enables us to interact with each other globally at unprecedented speeds. Apple's introduction of the iPhone in 2007 marked the beginning of the smartphone era. The tech industry embraced the moment in ways that precipitated a software engineering paradigm shift.

The companies that pioneered the design and manufacturing of smartphone products did so with a key assumption in mind: smartphones are as highly portable personal computation devices that are always connected to the Internet; i.e., digital technology had progress to the point where it was possible--and this possibility was treated as an inevitability--for everyone to be able to compute and access the Internet at any time and in any place. By 2014, the smartphone market neared saturation: nearly 90% of U.S. adults owned a smartphone proving the software engineering communities intuition was correct.

Researchers estimate that approximately 6 billion people worldwide (~72% of the global population) have access to the Internet and that there are between 1.34 and 1.49 billion sites on the internet as of 2026.

Aims

  • What is Digital Literacy
  • What are our Digital Rights
  • Who are the "The Magnificent Seven" and what do they each represent in the Digital Domain

Navigating

This section aims to answer two key questions: (1) in a sea of over 1 billion sources, how do you find what you're looking for; and (2) given what you find could have been created by any of six billion people, how do you assess which source(s) are the right/best for you.

  • How to find things: Search engines, recommendation engines, and "The Algorithm"
  • How to assess the quality/trustworthiness of things (sites, services, & apps)
  • How to identify misinformation / disinformation / deepfakes / "AI slop" / scams

Security

Just like in the physical world, sometimes bad things sometimes happen in the digital world. Some of those bad things are caused by people with bad intentions (i.e, hackers and scammers). This section provides an overview of the ways hackers/scammer perpetrate their malfeasances in addition to the tools (i.e., methods and means) we can use to protect ourselves.

  • Describe the cybersecurity landscape: threats, software/service/business provider roles
  • Define the role of **trust** in the cybersecurity landscape
  • Explain authentication and authorization in software systems
  • The role browsers play in maintaining your security
  • Strategies / best practices for managing your risk exposure
  • Survey of digital technologies/software in your cybersecurity toolbox

Privacy

When I am doing things online, I try to have a similar attitude/mindset as I do when I am in public: some things about my personal life I am comfortable sharing casually with anyone I interact with in public while there are other things I prefer to keep private. Maintaining my privacy boundaries in the physical world is relatively straightforward: I only talk about matters I wish to keep private with trusted confidants in discrete settings. Maintaining the same level of privacy when I am doing things online in the digital world (i.e., acting in the public digital sphere) involves modifying my behavior in different ways.

The primary aims of this section are to (1) to describe how things we do online can reveal more personal details about ourselves than when we engage in similar activities in the physical world and (2) inventory "digital tools/methods" we can use to help maintain our personal boundaries.

  • Explain behavioral data, tracking & data collection, and digital fingerprints & footprints
  • Maintaining your privacy boundaries on social media
  • Strategies / best practices for managing your privacy online
  • Survey of digital technologies/software in your privacy toolbox
  • The role browsers play in maintaining your privacy
